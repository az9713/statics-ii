Lecture 23

Introduction – Coulomb Friction

Setup and symbols.

We now add a contact law to equilibrium. N is the compressive normal reaction; F is signed friction along the contact. Their units are force. and are dimensionless static and kinetic friction coefficients stipulated for a model. W is weight; R is the total contact resultant. The angle measures its tilt from the normal, and is the limiting friction angle. Coulomb friction is an empirical idealization, not a theorem derived from equilibrium.

Static friction is an adjustable force with a limit

A rough contact can transmit a normal compressive force N and a tangential friction force F. For the elementary Coulomb model of a sticking contact,

The dimensionless coefficient mu-s is the assumed static-friction coefficient for the contact condition. The inequality is fundamental. Below impending slip, friction takes the value needed for equilibrium, up to its limit. It is not automatically equal to mu-s times N.

If sliding occurs, the simplest kinetic model uses a magnitude opposing relative sliding. Real friction depends on materials, surface preparation, lubrication, speed, temperature, and history. The coefficients in this course's examples are stipulated model inputs, not measured data for a named material pair.

Worked example: a horizontal push

A block of weight 500 N rests on a horizontal floor with mu-s = 0.4. A horizontal 120 N force pushes it to the right. Vertical equilibrium gives N = 500 N. Horizontal equilibrium requires a 120 N friction force to the left. The capacity is 200 N, so the block can remain at rest. Setting friction equal to 200 N would create an unbalanced horizontal force and would contradict the assumed static state.

At a 200 N push, the block is at the ideal impending-slip limit. At a 240 N push, no sticking equilibrium exists under the model. If mu-k = 0.3 and the block slides right, friction is 150 N left. Its subsequent acceleration belongs to dynamics; a static solution cannot be rescued by pretending the kinetic friction somehow rises to 240 N.

Fig. 5.1 — Forces on the block. Normal and friction act at the floor; the normal resultant reaches the forward edge at impending tipping.

Direction follows relative motion or its tendency

Static friction opposes the local tendency for relative slip, not necessarily the externally applied force and not necessarily the overall motion of a larger machine. A driven wheel can experience forward friction from the ground. In a wedge assembly, different contacts can have different slip directions. When the direction is uncertain, assign a signed tangential force and solve equilibrium before checking its magnitude and interpreting its sign.

The friction angle

The contact resultant R combines N and F. If phi is the angle between R and the contact normal, then

At impending slip, the resultant lies on the boundary of this admissible angular region. In three dimensions the analogous set is a friction cone around the normal. In a plane it appears as two limiting rays. The construction is useful for graphical reasoning, but its algebraic content is still the same inequality.

A contact can open

The condition N at least zero means an ordinary surface can push but cannot pull. If equilibrium requires negative N, the assumed contact is not active. A friction force at that contact cannot remain available after separation. This couples normal and tangential feasibility: friction capacity vanishes when the normal contact force vanishes in the elementary model.

Distributed contact and rigid-body equilibrium

A block rests on a finite patch, not literally a point. The resultant normal force can shift within that patch to balance moments. If its required location reaches an edge, tipping may occur before sliding. The first lecture isolates the basic force law; later lectures add incline geometry, tipping, ladders, wedges, and multiple contacts. In every case, start with equilibrium and then test the contact conditions.

Lecture 25

Coulomb Friction (cont.)

Setup and symbols.

A feasible friction force does not alone guarantee that a finite block stays upright. W is weight, b base width in the push direction, h horizontal-push height, P push magnitude, and e the forward shift of the normal resultant from the base centre. N is total normal reaction, not local pressure. The subscripts “tip” and “slide” label two different limiting values of P.

Editorial continuation: sliding versus tipping

A finite block can lose equilibrium in two different ways. The tangential force demand can exceed friction capacity, causing sliding. Or the required normal-reaction line can move outside the base, causing tipping. The first depends strongly on friction coefficient; the second depends on geometry and force application height. Both must be checked before naming the onset mode.

Let a rectangular block have base width b in the push direction and weight W acting through its centred mass centre. A horizontal force P pushes right at height h above the floor. Worked example: a tall block

Let W = 800 N, b = 0.6 m, and h = 1.2 m. The tipping threshold is 200 N. If mu-s = 0.4, the sliding threshold is 320 N, so tipping occurs first. At P = 200 N the required friction is only 200 N, below its 320 N limit, while the normal resultant has reached the right edge.

If the same floor has mu-s = 0.15, the sliding threshold is 120 N. At that force, e = 120(1.2)/800 = 0.18 m, still inside the 0.3 m half-width. Sliding occurs first. Increasing friction can therefore change the failure mode from sliding to tipping without making the block unconditionally safer against every motion.

Geometric criterion for the first mode

If equality holds, the ideal model reaches both limits simultaneously. A small change in geometry, coefficient, or load direction may decide which motion develops. The criterion assumes a horizontal push, a centred weight, and a level base. It should not be applied unchanged to an oblique force or an off-centre mass centre.

Why moments about the edge are useful

At impending tipping, the contact resultant passes through the pivot edge. Taking moments about that edge eliminates the unknown normal and friction forces because their lines meet there in the ideal point-contact limit. The equation becomes P times h equals W times b/2. This gives the same threshold with less algebra.

Before tipping, however, the normal resultant is not known to act at that edge. Placing it there for every load artificially forces the block into a limiting state. The unknown reaction location is part of the ordinary sticking equilibrium problem.

Contact pressure and what statics does not determine

Rigid-body equilibrium determines the total normal force and its resultant location. It generally does not determine the detailed pressure distribution across the base. That distribution depends on compliance and geometry. One can nevertheless reject a solution requiring the resultant outside the support footprint because no nonnegative pressure distribution over that footprint can produce it.

This is another example of feasibility reasoning: equilibrium produces a required contact action, and geometry plus the contact law determine whether that action is possible. The same logic will organize the ladder and wedge examples.

Why the normal resultant must stay within the footprint

Proposition: nonnegative contact pressure bounds the reaction location.

Editorial continuation: a ladder with two contacts

A ladder makes the interaction between force balance, moment balance, and friction particularly clear. Model a uniform ladder of length L and weight W resting on a rough horizontal floor and leaning against a smooth vertical wall. Its angle above the floor is theta. Why roughness at the wall changes the problem

A rough wall can also exert vertical friction. Then vertical equilibrium becomes N plus wall friction equals total weight, and the wall friction also affects the moment balance depending on the selected moment centre. There are more contact unknowns. Static friction laws are inequalities, so the interior equilibrium may not be uniquely determined by rigid-body statics alone.

It is generally incorrect to set both contacts to their friction limits merely because both are rough. A stated impending motion may justify a particular limiting combination, but it must be checked for consistent slip directions and feasible normal forces. Otherwise, determine a feasible range or use additional contact/compliance information rather than inventing an equality at every surface.

Reading the geometry correctly

The person distance s is measured along the ladder, so its horizontal moment arm about the foot is s cos theta. The wall normal's vertical lever arm is L sin theta. Mixing these projections produces an erroneous tangent instead of cotangent, or vice versa. Draw the force line and its perpendicular distance before simplifying trigonometry.

This ladder is a teaching model for equilibrium, not a field-use ladder rating. Its value here is that every force direction, moment arm, and inequality can be traced to a stated assumption.

Coulomb Friction (cont.)

Setup and symbols.

The wedge extends the interacting-body analysis to a slipping contact. W is guided-block weight, P the horizontal wedge input, top-surface angle above horizontal, N the upper-contact normal, and the stated limiting coefficient at that contact. Write . The floor and vertical guide are smooth. Friction direction follows the block’s motion relative to the wedge, which differs from the wedge’s motion relative to the floor.

Editorial continuation: wedges and interacting rough bodies

A wedge converts horizontal motion into vertical motion. Its sloping contact creates a large normal force, and friction changes both the required input and whether a load can drive the wedge backward. The main difficulty is keeping the contact forces opposite on the two bodies and assigning friction from their relative slip, not from the absolute direction of the applied input.

Consider a block constrained by a smooth vertical guide so it can move only vertically. A wedge beneath it rests on a smooth horizontal floor. The wedge's top rises to the right at angle alpha. The wedge is driven left by a horizontal force P, lifting the block. The upper contact has coefficient mu, the block weight is W, and the wedge weight is neglected for this example.

Worked example: lifting a guided load

If the wedge moves right and the block lowers, the block's relative motion along the top is down the incline to the left. Friction on the block is now up the incline. Its normal equation becomes N(cos alpha plus mu sin alpha) = W. The horizontal force of the block on the wedge is proportional to

For alpha = 10 degrees and mu = 0.2, phi is approximately 11.31 degrees. The wedge is self-locking in the stated model, with an ideal limiting rightward extraction force about 45.7 N. This conclusion changes if the contact coefficient, guide arrangement, or floor roughness changes.

A general method for multiple contacts

Draw each body separately. Define a tangent and a normal at every contact. Draw action-reaction pairs consistently. Propose the impending relative motion and assign limiting friction accordingly. Solve force and moment equilibrium. Then check normal positivity, unused friction inequalities, and any tipping conditions. If a check fails, revise the assumed contact state rather than retaining an impossible force solution.

This procedure is longer than memorizing a wedge formula, but it transfers to screws, clamps, brakes, and other mechanisms whose contact geometry differs from the textbook sketch.

Belt Friction

Friction can accumulate around a curved surface

A flexible belt or rope wrapped around a rough cylinder can sustain different tensions at its two ends. The larger tension side is called the tight side and the smaller the slack side. The rope's curvature creates normal contact force; friction associated with that normal force permits tension to change along the wrap.

Assume a flexible belt of negligible weight and bending stiffness, a fixed cylindrical surface, continuous contact, a constant friction coefficient, and impending gross slip. Radians are not optional

The differential derivation uses the small-angle relation with angle measured in radians. Substituting 180 into the exponent for a half-wrap would produce a meaningless enormous ratio. What the elementary formula leaves out

A rapidly moving belt can require centrifugal effects. A belt with significant stiffness may not conform ideally to a small pulley. Nonuniform contact conditions can require integrating a varying coefficient over angle. A grooved V-belt has a different normal-force relationship because of the groove geometry. Those cases should not be forced into the flat, massless, fixed-cylinder derivation without modification.

The essential idea is local Coulomb friction integrated along a curved contact. It explains the exponential dependence on wrap angle and connects the last topic of the syllabus directly to the first friction inequality.

This inequality is the aggregate capacity of the ideal model, not a prescribed friction distribution for every installation.

Example 4: a drum that is free to rotate

The fixed-cylinder assumption is easy to overlook. Suppose a rope contacts an ideal pulley on a frictionless axle, with no applied holding torque and negligible rotational inertia in static equilibrium. Taking moments of the pulley requires equal tensions on its two tangential sides. Contact friction alone does not permit an arbitrary unequal-tension static state if the pulley itself is free to rotate and has no torque balance.

The capstan inequality can still describe what the contact could sustain, but the pulley equilibrium supplies an additional restriction. For a fixed capstan, the support provides the balancing torque. For a freely rotating ideal pulley, it does not. A belt-friction solution checklist in prose

Identify which object is fixed and where the balancing torque comes from. Mark the actual contact angle, not the angle between the free spans unless geometry proves they coincide. Determine the intended slip direction, hence which side is tight. Use equality only for a limiting question. Convert angle to radians, solve the ratio, and combine it with any separate force or torque constraints. Finally, verify that both tensions remain positive and that the required reaction torque has somewhere to go.

Lecture 30

Course Wrap-up

Setup and symbols.

The final synthesis connects earlier models. P is a downward load, L the supported span, f the cable sag, and H its horizontal tension component. In the worked beam-and-drum system, T is cable tension, the lower end tension, and the drum-contact coefficient. Each model supplies one part of the solution; no single equilibrium result establishes both material capacity and contact feasibility.

One method, several mechanical models

The course has repeatedly used the same laws: force balance, moment balance, and action-reaction. What changes from topic to topic is the admissible interaction. A truss bar acts along its axis. A frame joint can pass two planar force components. A beam section transmits a force and a moment. A cable pulls tangentially and changes shape. A rough contact supports only certain combinations of normal and tangential force.

This suggests a powerful order for solving new problems. First identify the mechanical model. Then choose a body or cut that exposes the desired unknown. Only after that choose equations. Beginning with a familiar formula before identifying the model is how a cable gets assigned compression, a roller gets assigned a couple, or static friction gets incorrectly fixed at its maximum.

A unifying example: the same span, two different structures

A simply supported beam of span L carries a central point load P. Its reactions are P/2 and its maximum bending moment is PL/4. A weightless cable between equal-height supports carries the same central load with sag f. Its vertical reactions are also P/2, but its horizontal component is

The relation Hf = PL/4 connects the cable's sag to the beam's central moment. The beam resists the loading through internal bending; the cable uses geometric sag and horizontal thrust. Similar external loads do not imply similar internal force mechanisms or support requirements.

Four different kinds of limitation

A determinate equilibrium problem has enough independent equations to solve its force unknowns. A stable arrangement has the necessary geometric restraint. An admissible contact solution respects tension-only or friction restrictions. A strong-enough design has adequate material and connection capacity. These are distinct requirements. Passing one does not automatically pass the others.

For example, an overhanging beam can have a unique algebraic reaction solution that demands uplift at an unanchored support. A compressed truss member can have a valid equilibrium force but buckle under it. A symmetric spatial joint can balance one vertical load while lacking lateral restraint. Keeping these distinctions explicit is a mark of mature engineering reasoning.

A final worked synthesis

A 4 m beam is pinned at its left end. Its right end is supported by a cable running upward and left at 30 degrees above horizontal. A downward 6 kN load acts at the beam's midpoint. Moment equilibrium about the pin requires the cable's upward component to be 3 kN, so its tension is 6 kN. The pin supplies 3 kN upward and approximately 5.196 kN rightward.

Suppose the cable's other end wraps pi radians around a fixed rough drum with mu-s = 0.3 and is held by a lower tension. The minimum holding tension at impending slip is

The beam's maximum bending moment is 6 kN m at midspan. The horizontal cable component also places the beam in compression with magnitude approximately 5.196 kN. A later strength calculation would need both effects, not just bending. The drum support must sustain the torque caused by the two cable tensions, so it must not be modeled as a free ideal pulley.

This one system combines rigid-body reactions, beam cuts, cable direction, friction capacity, and a support-torque requirement. Each subsystem is simple once isolated correctly. The skill is to connect them without losing an interaction at a boundary.

Where to go next

Mechanics of materials explains how axial force, shear, bending, and torsion create stress and deformation. Structural analysis adds compatibility and stiffness to solve indeterminate structures. Dynamics restores inertia for moving mechanisms. Stability and buckling explain why an equilibrium configuration may fail under a small disturbance. Contact mechanics refines the rigid Coulomb model when pressure distributions and deformation matter.

For a computational extension, implement a small truss equilibrium solver that accepts joint coordinates and member connectivity. Have it report residuals, rank, and tension/compression signs, not just a force vector. Then deliberately introduce a mechanism or a tension-only member with a negative solution. A useful solver should expose those modeling failures rather than hide them behind a successful numerical solve.

The final assessment asks you to reconstruct these ideas on new numbers. Treat a correct free-body diagram and an explicit admissibility check as part of the answer, not as optional decoration around the arithmetic.

Notation is local when the physical model changes

Some standard letters serve different purposes in different chapters. The setup boxes define them locally; this register makes the collisions explicit. E is Young’s modulus only in the bar-extension discussion, while E elsewhere can name a joint. A is a cross-sectional area only in that same extension formula. C is a compression magnitude in trusses and toggles, a couple in the beam jump derivation, or a named joint when used as a point label. The beam-load gradient k has units of force per length squared; the belt ratio k is dimensionless. The catenary scale a is a length, while the beam point-load coordinate a is another locally defined length. The letter q denotes weight per cable length in Lecture 22, whereas qA, qB, qC in Lecture 12 denote force divided by bar length.

How to distinguish a law, a definition, and a derived result

Newtonian equilibrium is the governing physical premise here. The definitions of internal force, sign convention, or hyperbolic functions establish notation. Coulomb’s friction bound is an assumed empirical contact law. The two-force theorem, cable curves, capstan ratio, and force-reduction formulas are derived consequences of those premises. A proof box establishes a consequence within its assumptions; it does not verify the assumptions for an unspecified real structure.
