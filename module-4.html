Lecture 18

Introduction – Cable Support Systems

Setup and symbols.

A beam transmits a moment across its cut; an ideal flexible cable transmits only tangent tension. H is the positive horizontal tension component, T the nonnegative tension magnitude, and the segment’s tangent angle measured from positive x. The signed slope s = dy/dx uses y upward. At a downward point load P, are the slopes immediately left and right, both measured in the positive-x direction. They are not the directions of the two arrows on the isolated joint.

A flexible cable carries tension

An ideal perfectly flexible cable cannot transmit a bending couple or a compressive axial force. Its internal force is tangent to its current shape and pulls away from an isolated segment. Consequently, cable geometry is part of the equilibrium problem. A beam can resist bending while preserving an approximately prescribed shape; a cable must change direction so that its tensile forces can balance the loads.

If cable self-weight is neglected and loads are applied only at discrete points, each unloaded segment is straight. At a load point, adjacent segments generally have different directions and different tension magnitudes. If loading is continuously distributed, the cable shape is a curve. The correct curve depends on how the load is distributed: per horizontal distance or per actual cable length.

The constant horizontal component

Suppose all applied loads between supports are vertical. Isolate any cable segment. Horizontal equilibrium requires the horizontal tension component at its two ends to be equal in magnitude. Moving from segment to segment shows that one positive quantity H describes the horizontal component everywhere in the cable.

If a straight segment has slope s = rise divided by horizontal run, then its tension magnitude is

Thus tension magnitude is not generally constant, even though H is. The steepest segment has the largest tension for the same H. If a horizontal external force acts at an intermediate point, H can change across that point; the constant-H result relies on purely vertical intermediate loading.

A point load relates adjacent slopes

Take y positive upward and x positive right. Let the cable slopes immediately left and right of a downward point load P be sL and sR, both measured as y change per positive x change. The left segment pulls the joint left and with vertical component minus HsL. The right segment pulls right and with vertical component HsR. Vertical equilibrium gives

A downward load therefore increases the slope as we move from left to right. The left branch of a sagging cable has negative slope, the right branch positive slope, and the low point occurs where the sign changes or where the smooth slope is zero.

Worked example: a central load

Supports A and B are 8 m apart at the same elevation. A downward 12 kN load is attached at the midpoint, 2 m below the supports. Neglect cable weight. The left and right slopes are minus 0.5 and plus 0.5. Hence H times 1 equals 12 kN, so H = 12 kN. Each segment tension is

If the sag is reduced to 1 m, H doubles to 24 kN even though the vertical load remains 12 kN. This is the same shallow-angle amplification seen in trusses and toggles. A cable cannot support a transverse load while remaining perfectly straight with finite tension in this ideal model.

What information closes the problem

Support positions and loads do not always determine cable sag. One more geometric or mechanical specification is commonly needed: a sag value, a known point elevation, total cable length, or prescribed tension. Without it, a family of cable shapes can balance the same vertical loads with different horizontal components H.

The next lectures progress from one concentrated load to several, then to unequal supports and length constraints. The final lecture treats distributed suspension loads and distinguishes a parabola from a catenary.

The right segment’s positive-x tangent is proportional to (1,sR). Its force on the joint is therefore (H,HsR). The left segment’s positive-x tangent is proportional to (1,sL), but that segment pulls the joint toward the left: its force is (−H,−HsL). Add those two vectors and the downward load (0,−P). Horizontal components cancel. Vertical balance is −HsL + HsR − P = 0, giving the result.

The same sign convention works when both slopes are negative. In that case the joint can be above one neighboring point and below the other; guessing “both tensions point upward” would fail. The coordinate derivation avoids that guess.

Lecture 19

Cable Support Systems - Concentrated Loads

Setup and symbols.

Apply Lecture 18’s slope jump to one off-centre load. L is the support span, a and b its two horizontal load-point distances, f the downward sag below the equal-height support line, and S the geometric cable length. H is horizontal tension component; the two T values are segment magnitudes. A tension limit is a stipulated allowable magnitude in this exercise, not a measured strength or a complete design rule.

One load away from the centre

Let supports A and B have equal elevation and horizontal separation L. A downward point load P hangs at a horizontal distance a from A and b from B, with a + b = L. Its sag below the support line is f. The two segment slopes are minus f/a and plus f/b. Substituting into the slope-jump equation gives

The left support vertical reaction is Hf/a = Pb/L, and the right is Hf/b = Pa/L. These are the same vertical reactions as a simply supported beam with the same span and load location. The cable differs by also requiring horizontal reactions whose magnitude depends on sag.

Worked example: an offset hanging load

Fig. 4.3 — Cable geometry with vertical concentrated loads. Each unloaded segment is straight; the dashed line is the support chord.

Take L = 10 m, a = 4 m, b = 6 m, f = 2 m, and P = 15 kN. Then H = 18 kN. The left vertical reaction is 9 kN and the right is 6 kN. The tensions are

The shorter horizontal side is steeper because both sides rise by the same 2 m. It therefore has greater tension. At the load joint, the horizontal components cancel and the upward vertical components add to 15 kN. For the complete cable, the support moments also balance the applied load.

Reverse the question: allowable tension determines sag

Suppose the cable in the example must remain below an idealized tension limit of 25 kN. The steeper left segment controls. Its vertical component remains 9 kN for this equal-support, fixed-load geometry. Therefore,

Because H = 36/f with consistent kilonewton and metre units, the sag must satisfy f at least 36/23.324 = approximately 1.543 m. This is a statics-based minimum sag for the stated tension limit. It does not include a separate strength safety factor, anchor design, cable extension, or clearance constraints; those would be additional design inputs.

The inequality direction is important. Less sag requires more horizontal tension. A solution reporting a maximum sag from this tension constraint has reversed the physical trend.

Length and sag are linked

The total geometric length for one concentrated load is

If an inextensible cable's length is given, this equation determines f for the stated horizontal load position and equal support heights, after choosing the physically sagging branch. Once f is known, equilibrium determines H and the tensions. For the numerical example, S is approximately 10.797 m.

If cable stretch matters, geometric length and unstretched length are different. Tension then depends on both equilibrium and a constitutive relation for extension. Treating a substantially elastic cable as inextensible can mispredict sag and force, especially when the sag is small.

Why replacing the cable by one resultant is insufficient

The complete cable's force and moment equations find the vertical reaction split in the equal-height case, but horizontal equilibrium only says that the two horizontal reactions oppose each other. It does not determine their common magnitude. The cable's geometry provides the missing relation. This is an instructive example of equilibrium and geometry cooperating without invoking material stress analysis.

Editorial continuation: several point loads

For multiple vertical point loads, the cable is a polygon. Every unloaded span is straight, and every load point changes the slope according to H times the slope change equals the downward load. This offers a compact alternative to assigning a separate tension and angle to every segment.

Setup and symbols.

Editorial continuation: unequal supports and a length constraint

When supports have unequal elevations, horizontal reactions contribute to the whole-cable moment equation through the support-height difference. The vertical reactions are therefore not generally the same as those of a simple horizontal beam loaded at the same x coordinates. Begin with actual coordinates rather than importing the equal-height formulas.

Worked example: one load between unequal supports

Sag below the support chord

The straight chord joining A and B has equation y = 0.2x. At x = 4 m its elevation is 0.8 m, so the cable's distance below that chord is 2.8 m, not 2 m. The reference horizontal beam moment at that x is 36 kN m, and H times 2.8 equals 36. Thus the cable–beam correspondence still works when sag is measured below the support chord and the appropriate reference-beam construction is used.

The vertical coordinate and the chord sag answer different geometric questions. State which one is supplied before using a formula involving “sag.” Ambiguous sag definitions are a common source of apparently inconsistent cable calculations.

Sensitivity near a taut configuration

If the cable length is only slightly greater than the support separation, the sag is small. Small errors in length can then create significant relative changes in sag and tension. In the symmetric point-load case, let the excess length be epsilon. Expanding the length formula for small f gives approximately

Because H is proportional to one over f, it grows roughly as one over the square root of the excess length. This sensitivity explains why installation tolerances, thermal expansion, and elastic stretch may matter strongly in nearly taut cables even if they seemed negligible in a generously sagging example.

For a general multi-load cable with prescribed total length, use the slope-jump equations and support geometry to express all segment lengths in terms of H, then solve the length equation. The remaining equation is usually nonlinear, but a numerical solution should still be checked against equilibrium and positive tensions.

Cable Support Systems – Suspension Loads

Setup and symbols.

Replace discrete loads by a continuous load. w is downward force per horizontal length, while q is cable weight per actual cable length. H is horizontal tension component, x is horizontal distance from the lowest point, and y is height above it. A prime means differentiation with respect to x. The catenary length scale a = H/q has units of length. The functions and are hyperbolic cosine and sine.

Uniform per horizontal distance gives a parabola

Choosing the load model

Before selecting either curve, ask what quantity is uniform. A uniform deck load per horizontal metre suggests the parabola. Uniform self-weight per cable metre suggests the catenary. Mixed deck load and cable weight generally require a combined equation. Discrete hangers produce a polygonal cable model unless a continuous approximation is justified by their spacing and the required accuracy.

This distinction is the central lesson of suspension loading: similar-looking curves can arise from different load measures, and the measure must appear correctly in the equilibrium equation.
