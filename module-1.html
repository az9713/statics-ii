Lecture 1

Course Introduction and Topics

Before the first equation.

A free-body diagram is a drawing of one isolated body with the forces and couples exerted on it by its surroundings. The symbol means “add all contributions.” The quantities and are signed force components along perpendicular horizontal and vertical axes. The quantity is the signed moment about the chosen point O. We take right, up, and counterclockwise as positive in planar external equilibrium. The zero on the right of an equilibrium equation means the total is zero, not that every individual force vanishes.

What this course is about

A bridge, a pair of pliers, and a suspension cable seem to demand different theories. At the level of statics, they share one question: what forces must act so that every part remains in equilibrium? The difficulty is usually not a new law of physics. It is deciding what counts as a part, which interactions cross its boundary, and which mechanical assumptions are justified. This course develops that judgment by moving from connected rigid bodies to trusses, beams, cables, and frictional contact.

An engineering model is a deliberate simplification. A steel bar is deformable, but we may treat its geometry as fixed while finding its forces. A pin has a finite diameter and some friction, but we may replace it by an ideal joint that transmits force and no couple. Neither approximation says that deformation or friction is literally absent. It says that their effects are excluded from the present model. A result is useful only if those exclusions are reasonable for the question being asked.

Throughout the course, a force is a vector and a moment measures a force's tendency to rotate a body about a point or axis. In a planar problem, the basic equations are

These equations must hold for the complete assembly and for every isolated component. Applying them only to the complete assembly often finds support reactions but hides the internal forces that matter for sizing members and joints. Applying them to carefully chosen pieces reveals those forces.

The route through the syllabus

The first module teaches the separation of assemblies into bodies. The second specializes this idea to pin-connected bars, where the unknown interaction is only an axial force. The third extends joint equilibrium into space and then introduces cuts through beams. The fourth studies flexible cables, whose geometry must accommodate tension. The fifth adds friction, where an equilibrium equation must be supplemented by an inequality describing what a contact can sustain.

There are two numbering systems in the source syllabus. Its five course modules are the five files in this course. Its lecture titles also use the word “Module,” numbered 2 through 30 after the introduction. Here those are called Lecture 2 through Lecture 30, while their exact source titles are retained in the coverage table. This prevents a lecture numbered 23 from being confused with a twenty-third course module.

A complete solution has five parts

Begin by stating the system and the assumptions. Draw a free-body diagram with all external forces and couples. Write equations using a declared coordinate system. Solve for the unknown quantities with units. Finally, test whether the result is physically admissible. A negative axial force can mean compression; a negative cable tension means that the assumed taut cable arrangement cannot exist. These are different kinds of negative answer.

Use SI units unless a problem says otherwise. A kilonewton is one thousand newtons. A moment may be reported in newton-metres or kilonewton-metres, but do not mix the two without conversion. Mass and weight are distinct: a mass of 50 kg has weight approximately 490.5 N near Earth's surface if the assumed gravitational acceleration is 9.81 metres per second squared.

Force equilibrium and moment equilibrium are independent requirements

Two equal and opposite forces can have zero resultant force and still rotate a body if their lines of action are different. Such a pair is a couple. Therefore, balancing force alone is sufficient for an ideal particle but not for a rigid body. In three dimensions, rigid-body equilibrium requires

These are six scalar equations, although geometry or constraints can make some uninformative. In a planar system, only two force components and the moment perpendicular to the plane remain. Choosing a convenient moment centre changes the algebra, not the physics. If net force is zero, a zero moment about one point implies a zero moment about every point.

Let a force act at a position whose coordinates relative to O are x and y. Its signed planar moment is

This is the perpendicular component of the cross product of the position vector with the force. It automatically uses the perpendicular distance. A common mistake is multiplying force magnitude by the distance to its application point, even when that distance is not perpendicular to the force. The cross-product expression avoids that mistake.

Replace each support by what it can transmit

A frictionless roller on a smooth surface transmits a compressive force normal to that surface. A planar ideal pin transmits two force components but no couple. A planar fixed support transmits two force components and a couple. A taut ideal cable pulls along its own direction. A two-force link supplies a force along the line joining its two interaction points. These are modeling statements; the hardware's name alone does not establish the correct idealization.

Draw only forces exerted on the isolated body. If a wall pushes on a beam, the wall's reaction belongs on the beam diagram. The beam's opposite force on the wall belongs on a different diagram. Putting both on the beam would cancel the very support interaction being modeled.

Determinacy is more than counting equations

Three planar unknown reactions are not automatically solvable. Their directions must actually restrain the possible rigid-body motions. Three parallel vertical reactions do not restrain horizontal translation. Three reaction lines intersecting at one point may leave rotation unrestrained. Conversely, four reaction unknowns usually require information about deformation or support settlement in addition to statics.

Think of the equilibrium equations as a linear system. A unique solution requires enough independent equations for the unknowns. Geometric alignment can destroy independence. Later, space-truss examples will make this connection explicit through the rank of a direction matrix. For now, check both the number and the arrangement of reactions before starting a long calculation.

Lecture 3

2-Force Members and Multi-force Members; Introduction to Frames and Machines

Setup and symbols.

Using the equilibrium laws of Lecture 2, we now determine force directions before solving magnitudes. T denotes the assumed tensile force in a link; its horizontal and vertical components are obtained by multiplying T by the appropriate unit-vector components. are the pin forces on the bracket. A line of action is the infinite straight line through the application point parallel to the force. Concurrent forces have lines of action meeting at one point.

Why a two-force member has a known force direction

Isolate a body acted on by exactly two nonzero forces and no applied couple. Force equilibrium says that the forces are equal and opposite. Moment equilibrium about one application point says that the other force's line of action must pass through that point. The two forces must therefore be collinear. This conclusion does not require the body itself to be straight. It requires the stated loading conditions.

For a weightless link connected only by frictionless pins at A and B, the interaction direction is therefore along AB. Instead of assigning two unknown components to each end, we can describe the link with one signed axial force. If the link pulls its end joints toward one another, it is in tension. If it pushes them apart, it is in compression. A cable can supply the first action but not the second.

An apparently slender bar is not automatically a two-force member. Its own weight, a load applied at an intermediate point, or an applied couple can invalidate that classification. A straight bar with a transverse midpoint load is a multi-force member even if its ends are pinned.

Three-force bodies and concurrency

For a planar body acted on by exactly three forces and no couple, suppose two nonparallel lines of action meet at P. Their moments about P are zero. The third force must also have zero moment about P, so its line of action passes through P. The three lines are concurrent. The special alternative is that all three forces are parallel. This theorem is useful for finding an unknown reaction direction, but it must not be applied to a body with four forces or an unaccounted couple.

Worked example: a bracket held by a link

A weightless horizontal bracket AC is pinned at A = (0,0) m. A downward 8 kN force acts at C = (3,0) m. A weightless link joins D = (2,0) m on the bracket to B = (0,1.5) m on the wall. The link length is 2.5 m, so the unit vector from D toward B has components minus 0.8 and plus 0.6. Assume a tensile link force T acting on the bracket toward B.

The force at D is therefore 16 kN left and 12 kN up. Horizontal equilibrium gives . Vertical equilibrium gives , meaning the pin acts downward on the bracket. An upward link force greater than the external downward load is not a contradiction: its shorter moment arm requires it, and the pin supplies the balancing downward force.

Fig. 1.4 — Bracket free-body actions with the unloaded two-force link shown dashed for geometric reference. The solved pin vertical reaction is downward.

The bracket has three external forces: the resultant pin force at A, the link force at D, and the load at C. Their lines should be concurrent. Extend DB to x = 3 m; it meets the vertical load line at y = minus 0.75 m. The reaction at A points along the line from A to that intersection, since its vertical-to-horizontal component ratio is minus 4/16. Concurrency gives a second, geometric check on the algebra.

Why classification saves equations

If we treated the link as a general rigid body, we would introduce four endpoint components and then use equilibrium to recover their collinearity. Identifying it first builds the known physical constraint into the unknown definition. This is not a shortcut that sacrifices rigor. It is a rigorous reduction of the model.

However, it matters which body is isolated. The force the link exerts on the bracket is opposite to the force the bracket exerts on the link. On the isolated link, the force at D points down and right, while the force at B points up and left. The link is pulled outward at both ends. Keeping a sentence such as “force of link on bracket” beside an arrow can prevent a sign error that a bare label T does not reveal.

A useful distinction between structures

An ideal truss is assembled from two-force members loaded at joints. A frame supports loads and contains at least one multi-force member. A machine transmits or changes an input action into an output action and may have moving parts. In this course we analyze machines in a held configuration or under a quasi-static approximation: accelerations are neglected at the instant studied. Movement in the mechanism's possible motion does not prevent static analysis of its presently balanced forces.

Lecture 4

Frame Structures

Setup and symbols.

Lecture 3 classified individual members. We now separate a complete frame. are support-force components acting on the frame. are defined specifically as the right member’s force on the left member at hinge C; their negatives act on the right member. A hinge transfers force but no couple. The example coordinates are in metres, and load forces are in kilonewtons.

The complete frame hides its internal forces

When two members share a pin, the forces they exert on each other cancel in the free-body diagram of the complete assembly. They do not disappear physically. They become external forces as soon as either member is isolated. This explains both the power and the limitation of whole-frame equilibrium: it finds reactions without the internal unknowns, but cannot by itself recover all member interactions.

A reliable sequence is to sketch the whole frame, identify two-force members, find any reactions obtainable globally, and then isolate a member with few unknowns. Label a shared force once and draw its opposite on the neighboring body. A frictionless internal pin transfers force but does not transfer a couple between the joined members. A rigid welded connection can transfer a couple and must be modeled differently.

Worked example: a three-hinged portal

Fig. 1.6 — Three-hinged portal geometry. The two bent members are rigid; C transfers force but no couple.

Let A = (0,0) m and B = (6,0) m be ground pins. Two rigid bent members meet at an internal hinge C = (3,3) m. A downward 12 kN load acts at D = (1,3) m on the left member. Neglect member weights. The right member has no other load, but retaining its two hinge components initially makes the action-reaction bookkeeping visible.

This is the independent body check that would expose an action-reaction error.

What the result means physically

The right member is actually a two-force body because only its two hinge forces act. Its resultant at C lies along CB, with slope minus one. The calculated components have precisely that direction. Its bent shape does not change the theorem. Bending stress may exist within a bent two-force body because its material does not follow the line of action, but its two external forces must still be collinear.

The horizontal support reactions are often called thrust. They arise even though the applied load is vertical. The complete assembly sees zero net horizontal force, but each component needs a horizontal interaction to balance its moment. Global symmetry is absent here because the load is not centred; nevertheless, the sum of the vertical reactions still equals the total load.

A hinge changes the available equations

If the joint at C were rigid rather than pinned, an unknown connection couple would enter the separated-body diagrams. The extra equilibrium information obtained from the zero hinge moment would be lost. With the same two ground pins, this altered structure would generally be statically indeterminate. Material stiffness and compatibility would then be needed to distribute the reactions.

A hinge does not always make a structure stable. A badly arranged set of hinges can create a mechanism. Before counting equations, imagine whether one part can move while all ideal member lengths and support constraints remain satisfied. A frame can have enough unknowns on paper and still possess an unrestrained motion.

Including member weight

To include weight, place each member's weight at its centre of gravity on that member's diagram. A previously two-force member then usually becomes a three-force or multi-force body. Do not keep its force direction constrained along the endpoint line unless the resulting geometry happens to justify it. This is a frequent modeling error when an elegant weightless solution is adapted to a real structure.

For a complicated frame, a table of joint interactions can help: one row per connection, two signed force components, and one stated body on which they act. The table is bookkeeping, not additional physics. Every numerical result should still be recoverable from a free-body diagram and equilibrium.

Lecture 5

Machine Structures

Setup and symbols.

The separated-body method from Lecture 4 also applies to a machine held in a specified configuration. P is an input-force magnitude, Q is an output-force magnitude, and a and b are their perpendicular moment arms about the pivot. In the toggle, C is each link’s compressive-force magnitude and is its angle above horizontal. The differentials dp and dq are compatible small input and output displacements, measured along the associated force directions.

Mechanical advantage is an equilibrium result

A machine can trade displacement for force. A long handle may allow a modest input force to create a large force at a nearby jaw. For an ideal lever with perpendicular input and output moment arms a and b, respectively, moment equilibrium about its pivot gives

The ratio Q/P is the force mechanical advantage for the specified force directions and configuration. It is not automatically the ratio of visible member lengths. If a force is oblique, use its perpendicular distance from the pivot or resolve its components before taking moments.

Worked example: two independently balanced handles

Consider ideal symmetric pliers with a common frictionless pivot. Each handle receives a 100 N force perpendicular to the handle, at a moment arm of 180 mm. Each jaw contacts the workpiece at a 30 mm moment arm. For one rigid handle-and-jaw member,

Each jaw therefore exerts a 600 N force on the workpiece, in opposite directions. The workpiece's net horizontal force is zero. Calling its compressive load 1200 N by adding the magnitudes would be misleading: a section through the compressed workpiece carries 600 N. If a manufacturer defines “total gripping force” as the sum of two jaw magnitudes, that is a separate reporting convention and must be stated.

To find the pivot reaction, isolate one member and balance its input and contact forces as vectors. The pivot reaction is not needed to find Q because its moment about the pivot is zero. But it matters when sizing the pin. A calculation can correctly predict output force while omitting a connection load that controls the design.

Worked example: a symmetric toggle

Fig. 1.7 — Toggle geometry and input/output forces on the surroundings. Each compressed link delivers an outward force at its guided outer end.

Two identical pin-ended links meet at a central joint. Each link makes an angle theta with the horizontal. A downward input P acts at the joint. The outer link ends lie below the centre joint and press against horizontal guides; in the ideal equilibrium the links are compressed. At the centre, their upward vertical components support P and their horizontal components cancel. Let C be the compressive magnitude in each link. Then

Here Q is the outward horizontal force at each outer end. For P = 200 N and theta = 10 degrees, C is approximately 575.9 N and Q is approximately 567.1 N. At theta = 5 degrees, Q rises to about 1143 N. The large gain near a straight configuration follows from geometry: a small vertical component requires a large axial force to balance the fixed input.

The formula tends to infinity as theta approaches zero. A real mechanism does not deliver infinite force. Elastic deformation, clearances, friction, buckling, and actuator limits become important. At exact alignment, the ideal model is singular: finite axial forces have no vertical components and cannot balance a nonzero P. The infinite limit is a warning about the idealization, not a physical prediction of unlimited capacity.

Work gives an independent explanation

For a lossless, slowly moving mechanism, input work equals output work. If two jaws each move a small distance dq against force Q while the input point moves dp under P, then

The factor two counts two outputs; it is not an arbitrary modification of the lever law. This relation is a useful check when the mechanism's geometry provides the displacement ratio. It also explains why force multiplication does not create energy: the output travels less far. This course uses direct equilibrium as the primary method, while this small-displacement viewpoint offers a second route to understanding mechanical advantage.

Machines require a configuration and an operating assumption

A diagram of a linkage without its current angles may not determine its force ratio. Likewise, a rapidly moving mechanism generally requires inertia forces and dynamic equations. Our machine examples are held in equilibrium or treated as slowly moving with negligible acceleration. If friction is present at a pin or contact, its resisting action belongs in the free-body diagram; the ideal force ratio may no longer hold.

When checking a machine solution, identify the input body, the output body, and the interaction between them. Verify each member, not only the complete machine. Internal forces may be much larger than both the input and the net external load. That amplification is often the central engineering fact the analysis is meant to discover.
