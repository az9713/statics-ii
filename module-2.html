Lecture 6

Introduction - Truss Structures

Setup and symbols.

We now apply the two-force-member result to every bar in a truss. The integers m, j, and r count members, joints, and unknown support-reaction components. The signed quantity denotes the axial force in bar AC: positive means tension, negative means compression. A stable geometry restrains the relevant small motions; a determinate force problem has enough independent equilibrium equations for a unique solution. Those are separate tests.

The ideal truss model

A truss carries loads through a network of bars. In the ideal model, each bar is straight, each connection is a frictionless pin, and external loads enter at the joints. Member weight is neglected or represented by suitable joint loads. Every bar is then a two-force member, so its internal force is axial. This is the reason joint equilibrium works: the direction of each unknown member force is already known from geometry.

Real connections have finite size and may transfer some moment. Real roof loads can act between joints. The truss model is most useful when these departures have a small effect on the axial load path. If a substantial transverse load acts midway along a bar, that bar must also resist bending. Simply moving the load to a joint changes the local problem and needs justification.

A triangular arrangement is geometrically rigid in its own plane when its three bar lengths are fixed. A four-sided pin-connected loop can change shape without changing side lengths. A diagonal usually removes that freedom. This geometric difference explains why triangulation is common, but it is not a substitute for checking the actual support arrangement and member geometry.

Counting unknowns and recognizing the limit of counting

Let m be the number of members, j the number of joints, and r the number of unknown reaction components in a planar truss. There are two force equations per joint. A necessary count for a statically determinate stable truss is

For common pin-and-roller support with r = 3, this becomes m = 2j minus 3. Equality does not prove stability. Some equations can be dependent because of member alignment, and a redundant region can coexist with a mechanism elsewhere. A geometric inspection or a rank check is needed when the arrangement is unusual. If a stable truss has more independent force unknowns than equations, deformation compatibility is required to determine the forces.

The sign convention that simplifies every joint

Assume an unknown member is in tension and draw its force away from the isolated joint along the member. Keep that assumption consistently at both ends. A positive solved value confirms tension. A negative value means compression and reverses the actual arrow. The signed unknown describes the same member force at either end; the direction vectors are opposite.

Loads and member sizes are different questions

Statics gives the axial force demanded by a load case. It does not give the permissible stress or the buckling load. A tensile bar can be checked against tensile strength and connection capacity. A compressive bar may buckle even when its average compressive stress is modest. Therefore, identifying compression is more than a sign-labeling exercise; it tells the later design analysis which failure mechanisms deserve attention.

If all external loads are scaled by the same factor while the geometry and active supports remain unchanged, the forces in a determinate linear equilibrium model scale by that factor. This proportionality is another useful check. It fails if a cable slackens, a contact opens, or a different structural configuration is activated.

Lecture 7

Truss Structures - Method of Joints

Setup and symbols.

Using the model established in Lecture 6, isolate one joint. A positive member force points away from that joint. P is the downward apex load, L the total horizontal span, h the apex height, and the sloping bar’s angle above horizontal. C is a positive compression magnitude; T is the bottom tie’s positive tension magnitude. C and T here are magnitudes, unlike the signed member variables and .

Solve the equilibrium of one joint at a time

An ideal joint is treated as a particle. All member forces pass through it, so a moment equation about the joint is automatically zero and supplies no new information. In a plane, each joint provides a horizontal and a vertical equation. A joint with no more than two unknown nonparallel member forces is a useful starting point.

Before solving members, find reactions from the complete truss whenever that is convenient. Then choose a joint with the fewest remaining unknowns. Transfer each solved signed member force to the next joint using the appropriate direction vector. Continue until the requested forces are known. A joint left until the end often provides a valuable independent check.

Worked example: the triangular truss

Use the truss introduced in Lecture 6. At joint A, the known upward reaction is 5 kN. Assume both AC and AB are tensile. AC therefore points right and up from A; AB points right. The equations are

AC is in compression and AB is in tension. At C, a tensile AC force would point down and left, but its negative value means the actual force points up and right. Applying the same signed-force convention to BC gives . The two compression forces supply vertical components of 5 kN each and cancel horizontally, balancing the 10 kN load.

At B, AB pulls left with 3.75 kN. Compressive BC pushes down its member direction toward B, which is right and down from C, but the force on joint B is away from C: right and down. Its downward component is 5 kN and its rightward component is 3.75 kN. Together with the upward support reaction, the joint balances. This verbal direction check is useful because the same compressed member points differently on the diagrams of its two end joints.

General derivation: how height changes member force

For a symmetric triangular truss of total span L, height h, and downward apex load P, each support carries P/2. Let the inclined member angle above the horizontal be theta. The inclined force magnitude C and bottom tie tension T follow from joint equilibrium:

The tie force grows inversely with height. A shallower truss does not make the load disappear; it produces larger opposing horizontal components to obtain the same vertical support. For the example, L = 6 m and h = 4 m give T = 3.75 kN. Reducing h to 2 m while holding L and P fixed doubles the tie force to 7.5 kN.

This relationship explains a recurring structural tradeoff. Greater depth often lowers chord forces, but uses more space and changes member lengths, weight, and stability. Statics exposes the force trend; it does not by itself choose an optimal structure.

When the usual progression gets stuck

Some determinate trusses have no convenient joint with only two unknowns at the stage you reach. That does not automatically make the structure indeterminate. You can solve several joint equations simultaneously or use the method of sections to obtain one strategic force. The decision should reduce algebra while preserving a clear free-body diagram.

Write direction cosines from coordinates rather than from a crowded drawing. If a bar goes from joint i to joint j, subtract coordinates, divide by the bar length, and multiply by its signed force. This method extends directly to space trusses and avoids repeatedly guessing angle quadrants.

Accuracy and residuals

Carry exact fractions or adequate digits during intermediate calculations. Rounding a direction ratio too early can leave a noticeable imbalance at a final joint. A residual is the vector sum of all forces after substituting the solution. It should be zero up to numerical precision. A small residual caused by rounding differs from a systematic error such as using the same arrow direction at both ends of a member.

Each reaction is P/2 by whole-truss force and moment equilibrium. At a support joint, the compressed slope bar’s downward component is C sin θ; therefore C sin θ = P/2. Its horizontal component must be balanced by the tie, so T = C cos θ. Divide the two component equations to obtain T = (P/2) cot θ. Geometry gives tan θ = h/(L/2), hence cot θ = L/(2h), and substitution gives the stated result.

The first expression follows by using sin θ = h divided by the slope-bar length. As h increases without bound, C approaches P/2; it does not approach zero. As h approaches zero, both force magnitudes diverge, signaling the nearly unrestrained transverse direction.

Lecture 8

Truss Structures - Method of Sections

Setup and symbols.

Lecture 7 found forces joint by joint. A section instead isolates several joints and the connecting material as one body. Each cut member contributes one signed axial force, named by its endpoints, such as . A positive value pulls away from the retained portion. The reference point for moments need not lie inside that portion. The example uses metre coordinates and kilonewton forces.

A cut exposes only the member forces you need

The method of joints is efficient when many forces are required. If only one interior member is of interest, solving all preceding joints can be unnecessary. Instead, make an imaginary cut through members, retain one portion of the truss, and replace every cut bar by its axial force. The retained portion is a rigid-body free-body diagram, so it supplies two force equations and one moment equation in a plane.

Assume tensile forces pointing away from the retained portion. A negative result then means compression, just as in the joint method. The full truss reactions remain external loads on whichever retained portion includes their supports. Do not include forces at joints lying entirely within the retained portion: those are internal and cancel.

Why moment centres matter

If two of the cut-member lines meet at a point, taking moments about that point eliminates their unknowns. The third force can then be found directly if its line does not pass through the same point. The moment centre may lie outside the physical truss. It is a mathematical reference point, not a required support or material location.

The familiar advice to cut no more than three unknown members is a useful default, not an absolute rule. A cut through more members can still reveal one target force if all the other unknown lines pass through a common point. Conversely, three cut unknowns may not be individually solvable if their geometry makes the equilibrium equations dependent.

Worked example: a two-panel truss

Define bottom joints A = (0,0), B = (4,0), C = (8,0) m and top joints D = (2,3), E = (6,3) m. Members are AB, BC, AD, DB, BE, EC, and DE. A is pinned, C has a vertical roller, and downward 12 kN loads act at D and E. The complete truss is symmetric, so each vertical reaction is 12 kN and the horizontal reaction is zero.

How to choose the retained side

Both sides of a cut contain the same member-force information. Prefer the side with fewer applied loads, fewer unknown reactions, or simpler moment arms. If the smaller-looking side contains an unknown support reaction while the other side does not, the visually larger side may be easier. Efficiency follows the equations, not the amount of steel in the sketch.

After obtaining a section result, check one force through a joint if practical. Two methods agreeing on the same member provide stronger evidence than repeating the same algebra. Worked example: one load doubles

Let P = 18 kN and Q = 6 kN. The reactions are 15 kN at A and 9 kN at C. The formulas give DE = minus 8 kN, DB = minus square root of 13 kN, approximately minus 3.606 kN, and AB = 10 kN. The diagonal is now compressed rather than unloaded.

Substitute the values into the retained-body equations. Vertically, the reaction minus the applied load is minus 3 kN, while the compressed DB member supplies plus 3 kN. Horizontally, AB contributes plus 10 kN, DB contributes minus 2 kN, and DE contributes minus 8 kN. Both residuals vanish. The moment check about B gives minus 60 plus 36 plus 24 kN m, also zero.

If P and Q are interchanged, the top chord remains at minus 8 kN but DB becomes tensile. A member can reverse between tension and compression under different loading. That fact matters when choosing slender members, connections, or tension-only bracing.

Connection to beam behavior

In a deeper truss, a smaller chord force can produce the same moment because the separation between top and bottom chord forces is larger. In the simple idealization of two opposite chord forces separated by depth h, their couple magnitude is approximately Fh. Thus an internal bending moment M is associated with a chord force scale M/h. This is a useful estimate, not a universal exact formula for every chord at every joint. Inclination, panel geometry, and shear-carrying members affect the detailed result.

This connection prepares the transition to beams. A truss resolves internal action into forces in distinct bars. A beam distributes internal action continuously through a cross section. Both can transmit a net internal force and moment across an imagined cut.

Sections that fail and how to repair them

Suppose a cut passes through three member lines that all meet at one point. Taking moments there eliminates all three, so it cannot distinguish their magnitudes. The remaining two force equations are insufficient for three unknowns unless some additional information is known. Choose another cut, solve a nearby joint first, or assemble a larger simultaneous system.

A cut is a tool for obtaining independent equations. It has no special power merely because it crosses three bars. Always inspect the geometry of the force lines and ask which unknown each proposed equation will actually constrain.

Lecture 10

Truss Structures - Zero Force Members

Setup and symbols.

The section equations showed one way a force can vanish. We now derive local zero-force rules from joint equilibrium. A joint is “unloaded” only if no applied force or support reaction acts there. Collinear means lying on exactly the same straight line. P denotes the specified downward joint load; is the signed axial force in member CD. “Zero” always refers to the stated load case and idealization.

Zero is a precise result for a stated load case

A zero-force member has zero axial force under the particular idealized loading being considered. It may still stabilize the geometry, brace a compressed member, carry another load case, or carry self-weight omitted from the model. Therefore, finding zero force is an analytical simplification, not an automatic instruction to remove the bar.

Two common rules follow directly from joint equilibrium. At an unloaded joint with exactly two noncollinear members and no support reaction, both member forces are zero. At an unloaded joint with exactly three members, if two are collinear and the third is not, the noncollinear member has zero force. The qualifications about load and reaction are essential.

Derivation of the two-member rule

Choose one coordinate axis along the first member. The other noncollinear member has a nonzero perpendicular component. With no external force, perpendicular equilibrium forces its axial force to zero. Equilibrium along the first member then forces that member's force to zero as well. If the members are collinear, this argument fails: equal and opposite axial forces can pass through the joint.

Derivation of the three-member rule

Choose an axis along the two collinear members. Only the third member contributes perpendicular force. Its perpendicular component must vanish, so its axial force is zero. The two collinear forces need not be zero; they transmit equal axial action through the joint. This derivation also shows a useful generalization: an external load parallel to the collinear pair does not alter the conclusion about the third member, provided no other perpendicular force acts.

Worked example: a split triangle

Fig. 2.5 — Geometry and external apex load. Ideal pin at A and vertical roller at B. Coordinates are in metres; the drawing is schematic.

Let A = (0,0), D = (3,0), B = (6,0), and C = (3,4) m. Members AD and DB form the bottom chord, AC and BC form the sides, and CD is vertical. A is pinned and B has a vertical roller. A downward load P acts at C; no load acts at D.

At D, AD and DB are collinear and CD is not. Therefore CD has zero force. The remaining truss force solution is the same as for the unsplit triangle: each side is compressed by 5P/8 and each bottom segment is tensile by 3P/8. For P = 10 kN, those values are 6.25 kN compression and 3.75 kN tension.

Now move the same load from C to D. At D, the downward load must be balanced by the upward tensile force in CD, so . At C, CD pulls downward, and the side bars support it. The side and bottom forces happen to retain the same magnitudes in this particular geometry, but CD has changed from zero to P. A visual zero-force rule applied without checking the changed load would miss the entire load path into the truss.

Apply rules iteratively, but preserve the model

After identifying a zero-force member, it may be omitted from the force-balance sketch of a neighboring joint for that load case. This can expose a second zero-force pattern. Continue until no further deduction follows. Keep the physical member in the structural model when discussing stability or another load case.

Do not apply the unloaded-joint rule at a support unless the reaction is known to vanish or is included explicitly. Likewise, two members that look nearly collinear in a drawing are not exactly collinear unless the geometry says so. A very small angle can require a large force to supply a finite transverse component; replacing “small” by “zero” can fundamentally change the equations.

A zero force can be discovered without a visual rule

In Lecture 8, DB vanished under equal top loads even though its zero-force status was most directly obtained from a section equation. The local rules are sufficient conditions, not a complete classification. Symmetry or global load balance can produce zero member force in cases where no familiar joint pattern appears immediately.

Finish a truss analysis by stating the load case alongside the force table. Report tension, compression, and zero explicitly. A list of signed numbers without the declared sign convention is less useful to the next person who must interpret the structural behavior.
