Space Trusses

Setup and symbols.

What changes when the structure leaves the plane

A space truss is an assembly of ideal two-force bars whose joints do not all lie in one plane. The member force still acts along the member, and tension remains positive. The change is that each joint now supplies three force equations. A sketch projected onto a page can conceal the true geometry, so coordinates are usually more reliable than apparent angles.

For a member from A to B, form the displacement vector and divide by its length:

The three components of the unit vector are direction cosines. Their squares sum to one. This provides a quick check on a transcription error. The member's signed scalar force multiplies the unit vector pointing away from the joint under analysis.

Particle equations and whole-structure equations

At a joint,

A moment equation about the joint again adds no information because all forces are concurrent. For the complete spatial assembly, however, three force and three moment equations are generally available. A support system must restrain all relevant rigid-body motions. A collection of bars may be internally rigid yet inadequately supported as a whole.

For a spatial pin-jointed truss, the corresponding necessary count is m + r = 3j for a statically determinate arrangement, with support reactions included. As in the plane, equality alone proves neither rigidity nor independence. Coplanar or specially aligned bars can leave a joint unable to resist a force in some direction.

Worked example: a symmetric three-bar support

Let an upper joint O lie at (0,0,3) m. It connects to ground joints A = (2,0,0), B = (minus 1, square root of 3,0), and C = (minus 1, minus square root of 3,0) m. Each bar has length square root of 13 m. A downward 9 kN load acts at O. The ground joints are fixed in position by supports outside the isolated joint model.

Fig. 3.1 — Computed orthographic projection of the actual symmetric three-bar coordinates in Lecture 11. The dashed triangle marks the anchor plane; it is not three additional truss members.

By rotational symmetry, all three member forces have the same signed value F. Their horizontal components cancel. Their upward support must come from compression, since tensile forces at O would point down toward the anchors. The vertical equation is

Each bar carries approximately 3.606 kN compression. Its vertical component on O is 3 kN upward. The horizontal magnitude of each force is 2 kN, and the three horizontal vectors sum to zero because they are 120 degrees apart.

Why this example is more than a symmetry trick

The force directions were known before any force magnitude was calculated. That is the defining advantage of the truss idealization. If the same three elements were cables leading down to ground anchors, they could not support a downward load at O without another supporting action. The algebra would demand negative cable tensions. With bars, compression is permitted; with cables, that result invalidates the assumed system.

The example analyzes equilibrium of the upper joint with fixed lower endpoints. A full design would also determine forces on the ground supports and check each bar for buckling. The negative sign alone says nothing about the compression capacity.

How to organize a larger space truss

Create a coordinate table, a member-connectivity list, and a support list. For each joint, assemble its connected member unit vectors. Use one signed unknown per member throughout the structure. If all member forces are needed, a matrix solution is often less error-prone than a long hand sequence, but a few joints should still be checked independently. Computation removes arithmetic labor; it does not decide whether a support reaction or missing member was modeled correctly.

Three nonparallel bars can still fail this test if all three lie in a common plane through the joint. They then cannot supply a resultant normal to that plane.

For a small hand calculation, elimination is enough. For a numerical calculation, solving the linear system directly is preferable to explicitly calculating a matrix inverse. The important mechanical fact is not the computational method but whether the force directions span three-dimensional space.

A geometrically weak direction

Suppose all three bars become nearly horizontal while a fixed vertical load remains. Their vertical direction cosines become small. Large axial forces may then be required to create modest vertical components. The equilibrium matrix becomes poorly conditioned: small changes in geometry or load can cause large changes in the solution.

This is the spatial version of the shallow truss and near-straight toggle. Different-looking structures share the same underlying limitation: the available force directions have little component in the direction that needs support. Recognizing this pattern helps predict large forces before calculation.

Tension-only interpretation

If the elements in this example are cables rather than bars, the positive solution is admissible for the idealized joint. However, arbitrary changes in the applied load may make one solved force negative. In that event, remove the assumption that all three cables remain taut and analyze the appropriate active set, if any. A negative cable value cannot simply be relabeled compression.

The set of loads supportable by positive cable tensions forms a cone generated by the cable direction vectors, with the applied load opposite that cone. This is an optional geometric viewpoint, but it explains why cable-supported joints can resist some load directions and not others even when the linear equation matrix is invertible.

Dividing force by member length is useful, but it changes units. In the earlier equations the coordinate numbers were entered in metres. Written dimensionally, the x equation is . Thus qA = 0.5 kN/m, qB = 2 kN/m, and qC = 1.5 kN/m. Multiplying by the actual lengths in metres recovers the stated forces in kilonewtons. A unit vector is dimensionless; an unnormalized displacement vector is not.

What a small residual cannot establish

The residual measures how closely the computed forces satisfy the entered equations. It does not measure how accurately the entered geometry represents the real structure. For example, if all three bars have small vertical direction cosines, the solver can balance a downward load very accurately while requiring enormous axial forces. Check the direction span and force sensitivity as well as the residual.

Lecture 13

Space Trusses (cont.)

Setup and symbols.

Having solved a spatial joint, we now ask whether its solution is well constrained. The vectors are unit vectors along x, y, z. The cross product is the moment vector. A dot product with an axis unit vector selects moment about that axis. In the bar-extension relation, is extension, F axial force, L original length, E Young’s modulus, and A cross-sectional area; that A is not a joint label.

Editorial continuation: stability, checking, and whole-structure moments

A force solution is not the same as proof that a structure is stable. Equilibrium asks whether forces can balance in the stated geometry. Stability asks whether a small permitted motion or disturbance can occur and whether the system resists it. A structure can balance one special load while still being a mechanism under another disturbance.

Consider a joint connected to three bars all lying in the horizontal plane. Horizontal loads might be balanced. A vertical load cannot be balanced because all available member-force vertical components are zero. The joint also has an out-of-plane infinitesimal motion at that configuration. This simple example shows why a satisfied unknown count does not settle the question.

For the moment about a particular axis with unit vector e, take the scalar projection , choosing O on that axis. A moment vector may be nonzero while its moment about one chosen axis is zero. This distinction matters when a support restrains some rotations but not others.

A systematic verification procedure

After solving a space truss, reconstruct the vector sum at every free joint. Also reconstruct the total external force and moment of the complete structure, including reactions. Joint checks catch local sign and connectivity errors. Whole-structure checks catch missing external loads or incorrect reactions. Neither replaces the other.

Use a force scale such as the largest applied load to interpret numerical residuals. A residual of one millionth of a newton is negligible for a kilonewton problem; a residual of one kilonewton is not. For an ill-conditioned geometry, a tiny algebraic residual can coexist with highly sensitive force magnitudes. Therefore, residual and sensitivity checks answer different questions.

If the truss is statically indeterminate, member deformation and joint compatibility supply missing relationships. Lecture 14

Introduction – Shear Force and Bending Moment Diagrams

Setup and symbols.

We keep the cut-body method but now cut a beam. At section coordinate x, N is the axial-force resultant, V the transverse shear-force resultant, and M the bending-moment resultant. The function w is downward load per unit length. In the point-load example, L is span, a is load distance from the left support, b = L − a, P is the downward load, and are upward reactions. Superscripts + and − on a location mean values approached from its right and left, not positive and negative force.

What an internal cut means

Imagine separating a loaded beam at a location x. The material on one side previously exerted a distributed traction on the other. For planar equilibrium, that distributed interaction can be replaced at the cut by an axial force N, a transverse shear force V, and a bending moment M. These are resultants of internal stress, not three additional external loads applied to the intact beam.

A diagram plots how one of these resultants varies with x. It lets us find the largest internal demands and see where their signs change. The shear and moment diagrams depend on the external loading and supports. The conversion from these resultants to stress and deflection requires cross-sectional and material information studied in mechanics of materials.

Fix the sign convention before doing algebra

Take x positive to the right and distributed load w positive downward. On the right-hand cut face of a retained left beam segment, draw positive axial force to the right, positive shear downward, and positive bending moment counterclockwise. On the left-hand face of the retained right segment, all these actions reverse. Positive M is sagging: in the ordinary beam interpretation, the bottom fibres are stretched and the top fibres compressed.

Fig. 3.3 — Positive cut actions on the retained left beam segment: axial force right, shear down, bending moment counterclockwise. The opposite segment receives opposite actions.

The words “left face” and “left segment” are easily confused. Our positive shear is downward on the right face of the left segment. An infinitesimal element therefore has positive shear upward at its left end and downward at its right end. With this convention, the standard relationships derived in the next lectures are dV/dx = minus w and dM/dx = V.

Worked example: one point load

A simply supported beam has span L and a downward point load P at x = a. Let b = L minus a. Whole-beam equilibrium gives reactions RA = Pb/L and RB = Pa/L. For a cut to the left of the load, the retained left segment contains only RA and the cut actions. Its equilibrium gives

For a cut to the right of the load, include P at its actual position:

The moment is continuous at the point force because the load has zero lever arm as the cut approaches its application point. The shear drops by P. At x = L, moment becomes zero, consistent with the ideal end support. The peak moment occurs under the load and equals Pab/L.

Numerical interpretation

Take L = 6 m, P = 12 kN, and a = 2 m. Then RA = 8 kN and RB = 4 kN. The shear is plus 8 kN before the load and minus 4 kN after it. The moment rises linearly from zero to 16 kN m at x = 2 m and then falls linearly to zero at B. A maximum need not occur where a smooth shear curve crosses zero; here the shear jumps from positive to negative at the point load.

End supports and internal locations

An ideal pin at the physical end of a beam has zero end moment if no couple is applied there. A support located inside a continuous beam is different: the beam can transmit an internal moment across that location even if the support itself exerts no couple. Do not set M to zero at every roller symbol without checking whether the beam actually ends or has an internal hinge there.

When making a cut inside a distributed load, replace only the load on the retained segment by its resultant. Using the resultant of the full span would place some discarded loading back onto the isolated body and produce the wrong internal-force function.

Setup and symbols.

Use precisely the cut signs from Lecture 14. The short positive length dx separates two nearby sections; dV is the corresponding change in shear. The coordinates a and b in an integral are its interval endpoints; c denotes the location of a concentrated load P. Concentrated forces create jumps

A downward point force P at x = c creates . An upward reaction creates an upward jump of its magnitude. The left and right values are distinct at the idealized force location. Drawing a vertical connecting line indicates the jump; it does not represent a range of physically different shears at one ordinary cross section.

A pure applied couple does not create a shear jump because it adds no net vertical force. It will instead create a moment jump. Keeping these two effects separate is one of the simplest ways to avoid diagram errors.

The shear starts at plus 12 kN, reaches zero at x = 4 m, and reaches minus 12 kN just before B. The 12 kN upward reaction then returns the exterior shear to zero. The slope is minus 3 kN/m, exactly the negative load intensity.

Worked example: a partial distributed load

A 6 m simply supported beam carries 2 kN/m only from x = 1 m to x = 4 m. The total is 6 kN at x = 2.5 m. Hence RB = 2.5 kN and RA = 3.5 kN. The shear is

At x = 1 and x = 4, the load intensity changes but there is no point force. The shear is continuous at those locations; only its slope changes. It crosses zero at x = 2.75 m. That position will be a candidate for a bending-moment maximum.

An efficient drawing sequence

First compute reactions. Mark every support, point force, couple, and change in distributed load on the x axis. Starting from a known shear value, use jumps at concentrated forces and signed areas between them. Annotate exact values at each breakpoint and each zero crossing. Finally, check that the net load over the entire beam is consistent with the final shear value.

If your shear does not return to the required end value, investigate before integrating it into a moment diagram. A moment diagram built from an incorrect shear curve can look smooth and plausible while inheriting the original mistake.

Shear is a resultant, not a stress

The unit of V is force. Dividing by area gives an average stress scale, but actual shear stress is generally not uniform across the section. Its distribution depends on geometry. In this course, the purpose of the shear diagram is to establish the internal resultant accurately; detailed stress formulas should not be silently substituted for this equilibrium step.

Bending Moment Diagrams

Setup and symbols.

Lecture 15 related load to shear; we now relate shear to bending moment. M is positive sagging moment under the declared cut convention; dM is its change over dx. C is an externally applied couple, positive counterclockwise, at coordinate c. Here C is a couple magnitude, not the compression magnitude used in the truss chapter. A local extremum is a maximum or minimum relative to nearby sections; the largest absolute moment must be compared across the whole beam.

Applied couples produce moment jumps

Let a positive counterclockwise external couple C act at x = c. Moment equilibrium of a shrinking element around it gives

This sign follows our declared internal-moment convention. A clockwise couple causes an upward jump. The shear remains continuous unless a point force also acts there. Some textbooks draw opposite moment conventions; a memorized jump direction without a convention is unsafe.

Worked example: a beam loaded only by a couple

A 4 m beam is pinned at A and supported by a vertical roller at B. A counterclockwise 6 kN m couple acts at x = 2 m. Whole-beam moments give , so B acts downward with 1.5 kN and A acts upward with 1.5 kN. This ideal reaction at B requires a support that can hold the beam down; an ordinary unanchored contact roller would lift off, invalidating the assumed support state.

The shear between supports is constant at plus 1.5 kN. The moment rises from zero to plus 3 kN m just before the couple, jumps down by 6 kN m to minus 3 kN m, then rises to zero at B. The area under shear over the span is 6 kN m, but the applied moment jump cancels it. Integrating shear alone across a concentrated couple without inserting the jump would give the wrong endpoint.

Boundary conditions are physical statements

At a free end without force or couple, both shear and moment vanish. At a free end carrying a point force, the adjacent internal shear is generally nonzero. At a fixed support, an unknown reaction couple usually produces a nonzero end moment. At an internal frictionless hinge, the transmitted bending moment is zero, although shear and axial force may be nonzero.

To locate the largest absolute moment, check smooth points where V = 0, points where shear changes sign by a jump, both sides of applied couples, and relevant endpoints. Checking only the zero-shear points can miss a governing support moment or moment discontinuity.

Moment jumps require their own balance

Proposition: a concentrated couple changes moment, not shear.

A positive counterclockwise couple C produces with the cut signs used here.

Enclose the couple in an element of length Δx. The positive internal moment on its left face acts clockwise, contributing −M⁻; on its right face it acts counterclockwise, contributing +M⁺. Include the external couple +C. Moments due to finite shear forces are proportional to Δx and vanish as the element shrinks. Therefore −M⁻ + M⁺ + C = 0, which gives the jump. Vertical equilibrium contains no force contribution from C, so V has no corresponding jump.

On an interval without such a couple, the finite shear moment survives at first order after dividing by Δx; this is the origin of dM/dx = V. Consequently, integrating V across a beam must be supplemented by all concentrated-couple jumps.

Example 3: an overhang creates a negative support-region moment

A beam is pinned at A, x = 0, and supported by a roller at B, x = 4 m. It continues to a free end C at x = 6 m, where a downward 8 kN force acts. Whole-beam equilibrium gives RB = 12 kN and RA = minus 4 kN. A must therefore be capable of exerting a downward reaction; an unanchored resting support would not sustain this load case.

Between A and B, shear is minus 4 kN and moment is minus 4x kN m. Thus M(B) = minus 16 kN m even though B is a roller. Between B and C, shear is plus 8 kN and moment is minus 16 + 8(x minus 4), returning to zero at C. The beam is continuous over B, so there is no internal hinge and no reason to set its section moment there to zero.

A disciplined final check

For every example, the reactions balance the complete load. Shear jumps match concentrated vertical forces. Moment jumps match applied couples with the declared sign. The moment slope equals shear on smooth intervals, and load intensity fixes shear slope. Boundary values agree with the physical supports and end loads. Finally, every required contact reaction has the permitted sign.

These checks are complementary. Satisfying the differential relations does not prove the support reactions were admissible. Satisfying global equilibrium does not prove the internal piecewise functions are correct. A textbook-quality solution closes both loops.

At a spatial joint, three bar directions lie in one plane. Can they balance every possible 3D load?

Read the full solution

No. Their force vectors span at most that plane. A load component normal to it cannot be balanced. A special in-plane load may have equilibrium without proving full spatial restraint.

Problem 3

Foundation problem · Apply the model, show the equations, then check feasibility.

A roller lies inside a continuous beam. Must the internal bending moment there be zero?

Read the full solution

No. The roller supplies no external couple, but the continuous beam can transfer internal moment across its cross section there. Zero transmitted moment follows from an actual internal hinge or an appropriate unloaded physical end, not from an interior roller alone.

C3 · A smooth-looking plot with the wrong physics

Conceptual · Lectures 15–16 · Tests concentrated actions

Fig. 3.7 — Computed moment on a 4 m simple beam carrying a counterclockwise 6 kN m couple at x = 2 m. The moment drops by 6 kN m while the shear remains 1.5 kN.

A computed moment curve is continuous through a nonzero concentrated couple. Its author reports a tiny residual for dM/dx = V everywhere else. Explain why that residual cannot validate the full solution.

Read the full solution

The derivative law applies on intervals without concentrated couples. A counterclockwise couple C imposes an independent jump . An implementation can integrate every smooth interval accurately yet omit the jump entirely. The local differential residual then misses the defect. Check both one-sided values and the global end conditions, not only derivatives away from singular loads.
